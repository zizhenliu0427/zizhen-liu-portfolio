Working well with difference.

What Australian workplace culture has taught me about communication, inclusion and equal opportunity.

Zizhen Liu Individual presentation
Australian Workplace Culture & Diversity 01 / 05
Good morning. My name is Zizhen Liu. Today I will explain what I have learned about diversity in Australian workplaces. I will focus on two areas from my experience: culture and communication style, and language and accent. I will also explain the workplace impact, the relevant laws, and the practical strategies I now use.

Two examples from my experience

The same message can be understood in different ways.

01 · Culture

Direct and indirect feedback

In multicultural group work, I noticed that direct Australian feedback can sound strong to someone used to a more indirect style. I learned to check the intention before reacting.

02 · Language

Accent and speaking speed

Fast speech, local expressions and different accents can cause misunderstandings. A short written summary helps everyone confirm the same meaning.

Personal examples from study and group work 02 / 05
My first example is culture. In China, feedback can be more indirect, especially when speaking to a senior person. In Australia, people may give feedback more directly. At first, this could feel uncomfortable, but I learned that the intention is often to solve the problem quickly. My second example is language. In group meetings, accents, fast speech and local expressions can create confusion. I now ask people to repeat important points and I use a short written summary after the meeting.

Workplace impact

Diversity improves ideas and decisions — when people can participate fully.

  • +
    Better ideas: different experience reveals risks and opportunities others may miss.
  • +
    Stronger service: a diverse team understands a wider range of customers.
  • !
    Possible friction: stereotypes, unclear language or exclusion can reduce trust.
Inclusion turns difference into performance 03 / 05
Diversity can have a positive impact. People with different backgrounds can identify different risks, suggest new ideas and understand more customers. However, diversity alone is not enough. If communication is unclear, or if people make assumptions, the result may be conflict, silence or lower trust. The key is inclusion: every person needs a fair chance to speak, contribute and develop.

Australian legal protections

Fair treatment is more than good manners — it is protected by law.

2009

Fair Work Act

Employers cannot take adverse action because of protected attributes such as race, sex, age, disability or religion.

1975

Racial Discrimination Act

Protects people from unfair treatment because of race, colour, descent, or national or ethnic origin.

1984

Sex Discrimination Act

Protects against sex discrimination and sexual harassment, and covers attributes including sexual orientation and gender identity.

Sources: Fair Work Ombudsman · Australian Human Rights Commission

Equal opportunity means fair, lawful decisions 04 / 05
Australian workplace inclusion is supported by law. Under the Fair Work Act, an employer cannot take adverse action because of protected attributes. The Racial Discrimination Act protects people from unfair treatment because of race or ethnic origin. The Sex Discrimination Act protects against sex discrimination and sexual harassment. In practice, recruitment, training, promotion and dismissal should use fair and lawful criteria. Workers should also have a safe way to raise concerns.

What I do differently now

Inclusion is built through daily behaviour.

Australia

Open and formal

Equal opportunity is discussed openly, first names are common, and formal complaint pathways are visible.

China

More hierarchical

Communication can be more indirect, and raising concerns with a senior person may feel more sensitive.

My practical response

  • Ask instead of assuming.
  • Use plain language and written follow-ups.
  • Invite quieter people to contribute.
  • Speak up early and use the correct process.

These are personal observations, not rules about every person or workplace.

Respect · clarity · fair opportunity 05 / 05
In my experience, Australian workplaces discuss equal opportunity more openly and use formal processes. Communication in China can be more hierarchical and indirect. These are tendencies, not rules. My response is practical: I ask questions instead of making assumptions, use plain language, send written follow-ups, invite quieter people to speak, and raise problems early through the correct process. For me, inclusion means respect, clarity and a fair opportunity to contribute.
